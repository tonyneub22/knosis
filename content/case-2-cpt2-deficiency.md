**Chief Complaint**

“My legs are on fire again and my pee is the color of cola.” — severe bilateral thigh and calf pain with dark urine after an overnight shift.

**History of Present Illness**

Mr. Halvorsen is a 27-year-old man who presents with 14 hours of progressive, severe, bilateral proximal and distal leg pain, weakness climbing stairs, and dark brown urine. He has had two prior ED visits and one urgent care visit for similar episodes over 3 years; each was treated symptomatically and attributed to exertion, dehydration, or a viral illness. No episode was investigated for an underlying metabolic cause.

The current episode began after a 10-hour overnight shift in a refrigerated warehouse (approx. 2 °C) during which he had skipped dinner (“wasn't hungry, had a cold”) and drank only coffee. He had a mild upper respiratory infection for 3 days (rhinorrhea, low-grade fever to 38.0 °C). Near the end of the shift he noted aching in both thighs that progressed over several hours to severe, cramping-type pain and stiffness in the thighs, calves, and lower back. On arriving home he passed dark brown urine. His fiancée drove him to the ED. He denies chest pain, dyspnea, syncope, trauma, new medications, supplements, statins, or illicit drugs. He has not exercised in 4 days.

On directed questioning, he reports a lifelong pattern of prolonged muscle pain and “heaviness” after sustained activity lasting more than about 30–45 minutes — long hikes, soccer tournaments, and once after shoveling snow — that is worse when he has not eaten, is cold, or is sick. He has never had cramping or pain at rest, never had a “second wind,” and never had pain with brief intense exercise such as sprinting or weightlifting. He has noticed dark urine at least four times in his life that did not lead to medical care. Between episodes he feels entirely well and has normal strength.

*Teaching note: Recurrent non-traumatic rhabdomyolysis with identifiable triggers (prolonged exercise, fasting, cold, intercurrent infection), onset in youth, and normal function between episodes is the classic myopathic-form CPT II deficiency phenotype. In published cohorts, myoglobinuria occurs in ~86% of patients and exercise is the trigger in ~94%; onset is in childhood in roughly 60% despite the label “adult form.” Each prior encounter treated the episode as an isolated event.*

**Differential Diagnosis (at admission)**

1. CPT II deficiency (myopathic form) — favored: recurrent rhabdomyolysis triggered by prolonged exercise, fasting, cold, and infection; onset in young adulthood; asymptomatic between attacks; no second-wind phenomenon

2. Other long-chain fatty acid oxidation disorders (VLCAD deficiency, LCHAD/TFP deficiency) — distinguished by acylcarnitine species and genetics

3. McArdle disease (myophosphorylase deficiency, GSD V) — cramps early in intense exercise, second-wind phenomenon, abnormal forearm exercise test; not consistent with this history

4. Simple exertional rhabdomyolysis / dehydration — does not explain recurrence with modest exertion, fasting and cold triggers, or lifelong pattern

5. Viral myositis — intercurrent rhinovirus is a trigger, not a sufficient explanation for CK \>60,000 and three lifetime episodes

6. Drug/toxin-induced rhabdomyolysis (statins, NSAIDs, alcohol, stimulants) — no exposure

7. Inflammatory myopathy (polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy) — no persistent weakness, normal between episodes, no rash; serologies sent (negative)

8. RYR1-related exertional rhabdomyolysis / malignant hyperthermia susceptibility — considered given severity; genetic panel included RYR1 (negative)

9. Sickle cell trait–associated exertional rhabdomyolysis — hemoglobin electrophoresis normal

10. Hypothyroid myopathy — TSH normal

**Assessment**

27-year-old man with his third documented and at least seventh lifetime episode of non-traumatic rhabdomyolysis, now severe (CK 68,900 U/L) with stage 2 acute kidney injury, hyperkalemia, and hypocalcemia, precipitated by the combination of a 10-hour overnight shift in a cold environment, prolonged fasting, and an intercurrent rhinovirus infection. Metabolic studies demonstrate a long-chain fatty acid oxidation defect, and CPT2 sequencing confirms homozygous p.Ser113Leu — the myopathic (adult) form of carnitine palmitoyltransferase II deficiency. The diagnosis was delayed approximately 3 years across two ED visits and one urgent care visit during which episodes were attributed to overtraining, dehydration, NSAID use, and viral myositis. The recurrence itself — explicitly noted in the ED \#2 discharge summary — was the unrecognized signal.

**Plan**

**Acute management of rhabdomyolysis and AKI**

- Aggressive isotonic crystalloid resuscitation targeting urine output 200–300 mL/h; strict intake/output; Foley in place

- Continuous telemetry; treat hyperkalemia (calcium gluconate given for ECG changes, insulin/dextrose, potassium-binding resin); repeat BMP q6h

- Dextrose-containing fluids once potassium controlled — avoid fasting; provide continuous carbohydrate substrate (D10 at maintenance plus oral intake) to suppress lipolysis

- Trend CK, creatinine, and electrolytes q12h; nephrology consulted; dialysis criteria reviewed (not currently met)

- Serial compartment checks q4h for 48 h

- Avoid NSAIDs, nephrotoxins, and IV contrast; acetaminophen and low-dose opioid for pain

**Disease-specific management (metabolic/genetics and neuromuscular consults)**

- Trigger avoidance counseling: no prolonged fasting (\>8–10 h), no prolonged exercise without carbohydrate intake, avoid cold exposure without adequate fuel, early medical attention for febrile illnesses

- Diet: frequent carbohydrate-rich meals, bedtime complex-carbohydrate snack, low long-chain fat, medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) supplementation per dietitian; carbohydrate loading before planned exercise

- Sick-day protocol: oral or IV dextrose when unable to eat; written emergency letter for future ED presentations

- Discuss investigational options (e.g., bezafibrate, triheptanoin) with metabolic specialist — evidence limited

- Avoid valproate, high-dose diazepam, ibuprofen; anesthesia precautions — avoid prolonged pre-operative fasting and propofol infusions; inform surgical/anesthesia teams

**Occupational and family**

- Occupational health letter: recommend reassignment from refrigerated overnight shifts or structured meal breaks and warming periods

- Genetic counseling; offer carrier/affected testing to brother, parents, and the symptomatic maternal cousin (autosomal recessive; heterozygotes are occasionally symptomatic)

**Follow-up**

- Nephrology at 2 weeks with BMP; neuromuscular/metabolic clinic at 4 weeks; dietitian at 2 weeks

- Medical alert identification

**Key Teaching Points — Diagnostic Uncertainty**

1. Recurrent rhabdomyolysis is a diagnosis, not an explanation. A second episode of non-traumatic rhabdomyolysis, or a first episode with a disproportionate CK for the exertion, should trigger a metabolic myopathy work-up.

2. Trigger pattern is the key history: prolonged (not brief/intense) exercise, fasting, cold exposure, and infection point toward fatty acid oxidation defects such as CPT II deficiency; cramps within minutes of intense exercise with a second wind point toward glycogen storage disease.

3. “Generalized leg pain,” “muscle strain,” “dehydration,” and “viral myositis” are common ED labels that can each be individually reasonable while collectively masking a treatable genetic disorder.

4. Normal function, normal CK, and a normal acylcarnitine profile between attacks do not exclude the diagnosis; genetic testing (CPT2, starting with p.S113L) is the confirmatory test.

5. Diagnosis changes management: dietary and lifestyle measures markedly reduce attack frequency, and a written emergency plan can prevent life-threatening AKI in future ED encounters.
