**Why Patients Are Misdiagnosed**

An Evidence Review of Contributing Factors — Organised as an Inverted Pyramid

*Prepared September 2026 · Peer-reviewed and government-agency sources only · Percentages are reported as published, with their denominators, so they can be compared responsibly*

**1. How to Read the Numbers in This Document**

Diagnostic error research reports three different kinds of percentages, and they are frequently confused. This document labels each one so that the estimates can be used correctly in a survey, dashboard, or pitch.

- Prevalence of error — the share of patients or visits in which a diagnosis was missed, wrong, or delayed (e.g., “5.7% of ED visits”).

- Prevalence of a contributing factor among error cases — of the cases where an error occurred, how often a given factor was present (e.g., “cognitive factors were present in 74% of error cases”). These add up to well over 100% because errors are multifactorial: Graber found an average of 5.9 contributing factors per case.

- Relative risk / odds ratio — how much a factor raises the chance of error compared with its absence (e.g., “boarding patients had 1.6× the error rate”).

Three cautions apply throughout. First, most contributing-factor data come from retrospective record review, malpractice claims, or clinician self-report, each of which has known selection bias. Second, many of the best-known figures come from a small number of foundational studies (Graber 2005, Schiff 2009, Singh 2013) that are now 10–20 years old, though the 2022 AHRQ and 2024 UPSIDE studies broadly confirm the same patterns. Third, “no-fault” errors — where the disease was atypical or undetectable at the time — are real and account for roughly 7% of cases in Graber's series; not every misdiagnosis reflects a preventable failure.

Factors are grouped using the framework common to Graber (2005), the National Academies' Improving Diagnosis in Health Care (2015), and the AHRQ 2022 review: clinician cognition, the testing process, the disease itself, the patient, the work system, care transitions, and measurement. Those groupings are preserved in the concordance above; the layers below regroup the same entries by scope.

**Layer 1 — Structural conditions**

**Widest band, top of the pyramid.**

*Who gets believed, who can reach care, and the fact that none of this is counted. Operates over populations and years; present before any specific patient walks in.*

**D2. Demographic disparities and implicit bias — sex, race/ethnicity, age**

**What it is / mechanism:** Symptoms in women, racial/ethnic minorities and the young are more often attributed to benign, psychological or behavioral causes. Disparities persist within the same hospital, so access alone does not explain them.

**Best available estimate:** Female sex and non-White race were associated with 20–30% higher misdiagnosis risk across ED studies (inconsistently demonstrated, but never protective). Patients aged 18–45 with stroke were missed 6.7–7× more often than older patients. Black children with appendicitis were less likely to be correctly diagnosed than White children in the same hospital. Women wait longer for rare-disease diagnosis (OR 1.22). Stigmatizing language in the chart has been associated with diagnostic-error risk.

**Strength of evidence:** *Moderate–strong; effect sizes consistent in direction across many studies.*

**Key sources:** \[1\] AHRQ 2022 · \[19\] Newman-Toker 2014 · \[24\] Goyal 2020 (via \[25\]) · \[5\] EURORDIS 2024

**F2. Cost, insurance and resource constraints (patient side and system side)**

**What it is / mechanism:** Patient side: delayed presentation, inability to afford follow-up or specialist visits. System side: no access to MRI, specialist, or send-out tests; low- and middle-income settings with no laboratory backup.

**Best available estimate:** In high-income settings the direct evidence that cost causes misdiagnosis is thin and largely inferential; insurance status is often invoked to explain racial disparities but does not fully explain them. Rare-disease patients in Germany incurred 7.6-fold higher direct costs during the diagnostic odyssey than matched controls (€26,999 vs €3,561), showing that delay is expensive as well as harmful. The AHRQ review notes hospital-level variation in miss rates (e.g., MI 0–29% across hospitals), consistent with resource and process differences.

**Strength of evidence:** *Weak–moderate for cost as a cause; strong for cost as a consequence.*

**Key sources:** \[36\] German rare-disease cost study 2025 · \[1\] AHRQ 2022 · \[25\] KFF/Newman-Toker interview

**G1. No accepted definition or coding for diagnostic uncertainty**

**What it is / mechanism:** Clinicians routinely leave an encounter uncertain, but the record captures a single diagnosis code. Uncertainty is not codified, so it cannot be tracked, audited, or compared across countries.

**Best available estimate:** A systematic review of 123 articles found none defined diagnostic uncertainty; the authors proposed “a subjective perception of an inability to provide an accurate explanation of the patient's health problem.” Tools for measuring its communication are under-developed and not widely validated. Over 40% of chronic-pain patients who have a diagnosis still believe something remains undetected.

**Strength of evidence:** *Strong for the absence of a standard; this is the evidence gap your levels 0–4 framework addresses.*

**Key sources:** \[37\] Bhise 2018 · \[38\] Cox 2021 · \[39\] Communication of diagnostic uncertainty review 2023

**G2. Detection and reporting bias**

**What it is / mechanism:** Errors are detected mainly through return visits, autopsy, malpractice claims, and voluntary reports — all of which under-count. The AHRQ overall ED rate was derived from only three prospective studies (n = 1,758) outside the US, which is why it was contested by emergency-medicine organizations.

**Best available estimate:** Autopsy series historically show 8.4–24.4% major diagnostic discrepancies. Retrospective radiology discrepancy rates (~30%) far exceed real-time rates (3–5%).

**Strength of evidence:** *Strong that under-counting exists; magnitude uncertain.*

**Key sources:** \[40\] Critical appraisal of AHRQ report 2023 · \[35\] AJR review · \[4\] Newman-Toker 2023

**Layer 2 — The work system**

*The conditions in the building on a given shift. Operates over a department and a day.*

**E1. Crowding, boarding and patient load**

**What it is / mechanism:** High census delays testing, imaging and consultation and increases interruptions. The evidence is more nuanced than commonly assumed: boarding of the individual patient is associated with error; departmental crowding as measured by work score was not, in the largest recent cohort.

**Best available estimate:** In 250,049 ED encounters (2018–2023), patients who were themselves boarding had 1.60× the adjusted error rate (95% CI 1.42–1.82); ESI-1 patients 2.9× and ESI-2 1.5×. Higher ED work score (crowding) was not associated with more error. Earlier work (Pines 2011) did find an association between crowding and preventable errors. System-related factors overall were present in 65% of Graber's cases, dominated by policy/process, teamwork and communication problems.

**Strength of evidence:** *Moderate; mixed across studies, likely because “crowding” is measured many different ways.*

**Key sources:** \[28\] ED boarding, crowding and error 2025 · \[29\] Pines 2011 (via \[30\]) · \[6\] Graber 2005

**E2. Clinician fatigue, shift timing and burnout**

**What it is / mechanism:** Sleep-related impairment reduces attention and the analytic (System 2) checking that catches anchoring.

**Best available estimate:** Moderate, high and very high sleep-related impairment were associated with 53%, 96% and 97% higher odds of self-reported clinically significant error (OR 1.53, 1.96, 1.97). Emergency physicians spent 23.5% of on-shift hours at fatigue levels equivalent to a 0.08% blood alcohol level; night shifts had the highest fatigue scores. In physician self-report, 75.9% of memorable ED diagnostic errors occurred on the night shift after 5 PM.

**Strength of evidence:** *Moderate; self-report of error is the main outcome in most studies.*

**Key sources:** \[31\] Trockel/physician sleep study · \[32\] Objective EP fatigue study 2022 · \[26\] Japanese ED study

**E4. Information overload, EHR design and alert fatigue**

**What it is / mechanism:** Fragmented records, copy-forward notes, and high alert volumes bury the signal.

**Best available estimate:** 56 alerts per clinician per day; 30% missed at least one in the past year (VA). Retrospective radiology error rates ~30% versus 3–5% real-time, attributed in part to rising workload.

**Strength of evidence:** *Moderate.*

**Key sources:** \[17\] VA alert survey 2013 · \[35\] AJR radiology error review

**Layer 3 — Continuity and transitions**

*What is lost in the gaps between encounters. Operates over weeks and multiple visits.*

**E3. Communication and handoff failures**

**What it is / mechanism:** Information lost between shifts, between ED and inpatient team, between hospital and outpatient care, or between clinician and patient.

**Best available estimate:** An estimated 67% of communication errors relate to handoffs. Structured handoff programs (I-PASS) reduced medical errors by 23% and preventable adverse events by 30% in a multicenter trial. In UPSIDE, process failures in subspecialty consultation and patient experience were independently associated with harmful errors.

**Strength of evidence:** *Strong for the effect of handoff programs; the 67% figure is from Joint Commission analysis.*

**Key sources:** \[33\] Joint Commission 2024 · \[34\] Starmer 2014 NEJM · \[3\] Auerbach 2024

**B2. Abnormal results not followed up (“lost” results)**

**What it is / mechanism:** A result returns abnormal after the encounter and no one acts on it — most common at ED discharge, hospital discharge, and when tests are pending at discharge. This is the measurable core of your “no PCP follow-up” item.

**Best available estimate:** A systematic review of 19 ambulatory studies found 6.8% to 62% of laboratory results and 1.0% to 35.7% of radiology results were not followed up. In a VA survey of 2,590 primary-care clinicians, 30% reported missing a test-result alert in the past year; clinicians received an average of 56 alerts per day and 55% said the notification system made it possible to miss results.

**Strength of evidence:** *Strong for the range; the range itself is wide because definitions differ.*

**Key sources:** \[16\] Callen 2011 · \[17\] Singh/VA alert survey 2013 · \[18\] Test-result follow-up implementation gaps 2021

**F1. No primary-care home / failed transitions**

**What it is / mechanism:** Without a longitudinal clinician, no one integrates repeated acute visits into a pattern, and pending results have no owner. Transition points (ED discharge, hospital discharge) are the highest-risk moments for lost results.

**Best available estimate:** Direct quantification is mostly through the test-follow-up literature (see B2). Rare-disease data show the number of clinicians consulted is the strongest determinant of delay (OR 5.15), consistent with fragmentation driving delay.

**Strength of evidence:** *Moderate; mechanism well supported, direct percentage for “no PCP” as a cause is not established.*

**Key sources:** \[16\] Callen 2011 · \[5\] EURORDIS 2024 · \[18\] Implementation-gaps study 2021

**Layer 4 — The encounter**

*One visit, one conversation. Operates over twenty minutes.*

**A3. Faulty data gathering — inadequate history and examination**

**What it is / mechanism:** Not eliciting or not weighing key history (timeline, triggers, prior episodes, prior visits) and incomplete examination. This is where a patient's “non-detailed history” interacts with clinician technique: most breakdowns were judged to be in elicitation and synthesis rather than patient concealment.

**Best available estimate:** In 190 confirmed primary-care errors, breakdowns in the patient–clinician encounter occurred in 78.9%; specifically history-taking 56.3%, physical examination 47.4%, and ordering diagnostic tests 57.4%. In Schiff's 583 errors, history accounted for 10% and exam 10% of the primary failure point. In UPSIDE (hospitalized patients), patient-assessment problems had the highest attributable fraction: eliminating them would remove an estimated 21.4% of errors.

**Strength of evidence:** *Strong and consistent across outpatient and inpatient settings.*

**Key sources:** \[13\] Singh 2013 · \[14\] Schiff 2009 · \[3\] Auerbach 2024

**D1. Communication barriers — language, health literacy, ability to give a history**

**What it is / mechanism:** Limited English proficiency, use of family members as interpreters, cognitive impairment, intoxication, or psychiatric symptoms that make the patient's account harder to obtain or easier to discount.

**Best available estimate:** Patient-related factors were present in 44% of Graber's error cases. Among limited-English-proficiency inpatients, 52.4% of adverse events were due to communication errors versus 35.9% for English speakers, and 49.1% involved physical harm versus 29.5%. Language-concordant care reduced adverse events (adjusted OR 0.25) and in-hospital death (OR 0.44) in an ED cohort.

**Strength of evidence:** *Moderate–strong; direct diagnostic-error data for LEP are limited, most evidence is for adverse events generally.*

**Key sources:** \[6\] Graber 2005 · \[21\] Divi 2007 · \[22\] Language-concordant ED care study (via \[23\])

**B1. Failure to order the right test, misinterpretation, or wrong test**

**What it is / mechanism:** The right test is not ordered (or is deferred), the result is misread, or a normal test falsely reassures. Includes false-negative imaging (e.g., CT misses \>80% of acute ischemic strokes in the first 48 hours).

**Best available estimate:** Testing was the most common failure point in Schiff's 583 physician-reported errors (44%, including ordering, reporting and follow-up), ahead of clinician assessment (32%). In UPSIDE, test ordering and interpretation problems had an attributable fraction of 19.9%; together with assessment problems, eliminating both would cut errors by ~40%. In Singh's primary-care series, test-ordering problems were present in 57.4% of errors.

**Strength of evidence:** *Strong; replicated in inpatient, outpatient and ED settings.*

**Key sources:** \[14\] Schiff 2009 · \[3\] Auerbach 2024 · \[13\] Singh 2013 · \[1\] AHRQ 2022

**Layer 5 — The case itself**

*What this particular patient's presentation makes possible. Operates over one patient's problem.*

**C1. Atypical, non-specific, or early presentation**

**What it is / mechanism:** Symptoms that overlap with benign, common conditions (dizziness, headache, fatigue, myalgia, behavioral change) are the strongest disease-side predictor of error. Roughly 95% of patients with such symptoms in acute care do not have the dangerous disease, so the base rate works against detection.

**Best available estimate:** For stroke, dizziness/vertigo raises the odds of misdiagnosis 14-fold compared with motor weakness (40% missed vs 4%). Spinal abscess is missed 56% of the time; MI only 1.5%. Overall stroke miss rate ~17%, about 10× that of MI despite comparable incidence. Up to 12.7% of patients later admitted for stroke had been sent home from an ED in the preceding 30 days, mostly with dizziness or headache.

**Strength of evidence:** *Strong; large population datasets.*

**Key sources:** \[1\] AHRQ 2022 · \[19\] Newman-Toker 2014 (Diagnosis)

**C2. Rare disease and the diagnostic odyssey**

**What it is / mechanism:** Low prevalence plus non-specific symptoms plus few clinicians having ever seen a case. Misdiagnosis as a psychiatric condition is particularly common.

**Best available estimate:** Average time to diagnosis 4.7 years across 1,675 rare diseases; 56% diagnosed more than 6 months after first medical contact; 60% first misdiagnosed with another physical disease and 60% with a psychological condition or dismissed. Determinants of delay: symptom onset in childhood (OR 3.1) or adolescence (OR 4.8), being a woman (OR 1.22), number of clinicians consulted (OR 5.15), any misdiagnosis (OR 2.48). Referral to a center of expertise shortened the journey by 1.1 years. A US survey of 3,471 patients found 46% received a misdiagnosis and patients saw an average of four physicians and had seven tests before diagnosis.

**Strength of evidence:** *Strong for magnitude (large surveys); patient-reported, so misdiagnosis is defined by the patient.*

**Key sources:** \[5\] EURORDIS/Faye 2024 · \[20\] US rare-disease patient survey (Rare Patient Voice / SWAN) · \[15\] Clinician survey 2023

**D3. Prior visits and prior labels (“bounce-back” patients)**

**What it is / mechanism:** A patient who has already been seen and labeled is at higher risk of the label being carried forward — the previous diagnosis becomes an anchor. Return visits are also the main way diagnostic errors are detected, so this is both a cause and a signal.

**Best available estimate:** In ED-based self-reported error cases, the most common initial misdiagnoses were common conditions in the same organ system as the missed disease (e.g., “primary headache” → stroke in 80% of those errors; “upper GI disease” → obstruction/peritonitis in 27%). Formal quantification of the added risk from a prior benign label is limited; the Ly 2023 JAMA Internal Medicine study demonstrated that a triage label of CHF measurably reduced PE testing in dyspneic patients (anchoring in a natural experiment).

**Strength of evidence:** *Moderate; direct estimates of risk conferred by a prior ED visit are sparse — this is a gap your project could fill.*

**Key sources:** \[26\] Japanese ED cognitive-bias study 2022 · \[27\] Ly 2023

**Layer 6 — The reasoning moment**

**The tip.**

*One clinician, one decision to stop considering alternatives. Operates over seconds.*

*Present in roughly three-quarters of diagnostic errors in every major record-review study. Importantly, these are mostly failures of synthesis, not of knowledge.*

**A1. Premature closure and anchoring**

**What it is / mechanism:** Settling on a diagnosis early and failing to keep considering alternatives (premature closure), or fixing on an initial impression — often from triage notes, a prior visit, or the chief complaint — and interpreting later data through that lens (anchoring). Both are amplified by time pressure and by a prior label such as “anxiety” or “muscle strain.”

**Best available estimate:** Cognitive factors present in 74% of 100 internal-medicine errors (Graber); premature closure was the single most common cause. In a self-reflection survey of 130 Japanese physicians, anchoring was reported in 60.0% and premature closure in 58.5% of memorable error cases. A systematic review (Saposnik) found cognitive biases associated with diagnostic error in 36.5–77% of case studies. In simulation, first-year residents showed premature closure in 78.6% and anchoring in 75.7% of scenarios.

**Strength of evidence:** *Consistent across record review, self-report, simulation and systematic review; magnitude varies by method.*

**Key sources:** \[6\] Graber 2005 · \[7\] Watari 2022 · \[8\] Saposnik 2016 (via \[9\]) · \[10\] Sinha simulation study

**A2. Availability and representativeness heuristics**

**What it is / mechanism:** Judging likelihood by how easily a diagnosis comes to mind (recent cases, common diseases) or by how closely a patient resembles a mental prototype. Drives “common things are common” reasoning that misses rare disease and atypical presentations.

**Best available estimate:** Availability bias reported in 46.2% of self-reported error cases; “hassle bias” (avoiding a work-up that is inconvenient) in 33.1%. Physicians attributed an average of 3.08 biases to each error.

**Strength of evidence:** *Moderate — self-report; experimental studies confirm availability bias can be induced and is neutralized by reflective reasoning.*

**Key sources:** \[7\] Watari 2022 · \[11\] Mamede/Schmidt experimental work (summarized in \[12\])

**A4. Knowledge deficits (disease-specific education gaps)**

**What it is / mechanism:** Not knowing the presentation, triggers, or red flags of a specific condition. Ranks low for common disease in specialist settings but high for rare disease.

**Best available estimate:** Faulty or inadequate knowledge was “uncommon” in Graber's series — about 4% of cognitive factors, or 3% of all factors — and was concentrated in rare conditions. Conversely, in a multinational survey of clinicians on rare disease, 38–44% reported barriers in knowledge of signs/symptoms, time to investigate, guideline availability, test access, and referral; only 19% were mostly or very confident making a rare-disease diagnosis, and 59% said they never or rarely see one despite a population prevalence of 3.5–5.9%.

**Strength of evidence:** *Strong for the general claim (knowledge is a minor factor overall); moderate (survey-based) for the rare-disease claim.*

**Key sources:** \[6\] Graber 2005 · \[15\] Rare-disease clinician survey 2023

**8. Implications for a Levels 0–4 Diagnostic-Uncertainty Tool**

- The literature's largest single lever is clinician synthesis (premature closure/anchoring), which is exactly what a forced uncertainty rating interrupts. A tool that requires the rater to say “how sure am I?” before discharge is a recognized debiasing strategy.

- Vignettes with atypical presentations (dizziness, headache, myalgia, behavioral change) and with a prior benign ED label are where the measured miss rates are highest; those should be over-represented in a test set.

- Recording rater role, sex, and region allows you to test the demographic and geographic disparity findings above, which are currently inconsistent across studies.

- Helene's hypothesis — that recently discharged ED patients carry more diagnostic uncertainty — is consistent with the return-visit literature but has not been directly quantified; a vignette-rating study is a reasonable first test.

- Because no standard definition of diagnostic uncertainty exists, publishing the level descriptions with inter-rater agreement statistics would itself be a contribution.

**Appendix A — Summary Notes Carried Over From the Working Document**

*These notes were appended to the working copy of this review and are reproduced here unchanged so nothing is lost.*

The document is ~11 pages, 41 references, structured in seven sections. Headlines from the evidence, and how it changes your list:

**Biggest edit: add cognitive factors.** They're the single most common contributor in every major study — present in 74% of internal-medicine errors (Graber 2005), with premature closure the top single cause and anchoring self-reported in 60% of cases. Your list had none of this.

**Your items, revised:**

- Wait time/patient load → split. Boarding raises a patient's error rate 1.6×, but departmental crowding wasn't associated with error in the largest cohort (250k encounters). Fatigue has its own evidence: sleep-impaired physicians have 1.5–2× the odds of significant error.

- Non-detailed patient history → reframe as clinician data-gathering. History breakdowns are in 56% of primary-care errors, but reviewers put most of it on elicitation, not the patient. Language barriers get their own line (52% of LEP adverse events are communication-related vs 36%).

- No PCP follow-up → broaden to test-result closure. 6.8–62% of ambulatory lab results go un-followed; that's the measurable core.

- ICD/definition of uncertainty → this isn't a cause of misdiagnosis in any study. It's a measurement barrier — Bhise's systematic review found none of 123 papers defined diagnostic uncertainty. That's actually your strongest argument for the levels 0–4 framework, so I moved it to its own section rather than deleting it.

- Disease education → knowledge deficits are only ~3–4% of factors for common disease, but 38–44% of clinicians cite them for rare disease.

- Money/resources → direct evidence that cost causes misdiagnosis is thin; evidence that delay costs money is strong (7.6× higher spend during rare-disease odysseys).

**Also added:** atypical presentation (dizziness raises stroke-miss odds 14×), demographics (women/non-White +20–30%; young stroke patients missed 7×), testing failures (44% of Schiff's 583 errors), and handoffs.

One caveat I flagged in the document: percentages come from three different kinds of denominators (prevalence, presence-among-errors, relative risk), and I labeled each so they aren't compared as if they were the same thing.
